## 1. Pre-close call (10 minutes, before the offer)

Call her Monday morning. Frame it as "before we put an offer together, I want to make sure it fits." Don't name a number yet except in question 6.

1. **"Where else are you in process, and when do you expect to hear?"** Listening for: the date of the big company's final round and offer, and whether "end of next week" means 2026-10-09 or 2026-10-16. Don't press for the company name; she has already declined to give it.
2. **"If you had an offer from us and from them, how would you decide? What are your top 2 or 3 things?"** Listening for: her words. You already expect "owning a product area" and "runway". Write down exactly how she says them; the offer call answers each one.
3. **"What compensation are you looking for?"** Listening for: whether "around $180k" still holds, and whether she frames it as base or total. Expectation only, never what she earns now.
4. **"You've asked about our runway a couple of times. What would you need to see or hear to feel good about it?"** Listening for: whether she wants months of runway, burn, the board, or a talk with your CFO. Offer that conversation; it is a decision criterion, not a side question.
5. **"Who else is part of this decision?"** Listening for: a partner or mentor. Offer them a call with you or a future teammate.
6. **"If your current company offered you more to stay, what would you do?"** Listening for: hesitation. If she isn't sure, ask what made her start looking and write it down. Also listen for whether she raises the February bonus herself; if she does, note it, but don't ask what it's worth.
7. **"If we came in at the high $170s base, with a sign-on and a start after Thanksgiving, would that be in the zone, or would something get in the way?"** Listening for: "yes" or the real objection, before you commit to numbers.

## 3. Verbal offer script

Founder calls by phone or video, Monday afternoon. Not email first.

**Opening.** "Ana, thanks for making time. I'm calling with good news: the whole team was a strong yes, and we'd like you to join Northbay Grid as our sixth engineer."

**Why her (2-3 specifics).** "A few things stood out. [specific 1 from the interviews, e.g. something she built or explained]. [specific 2]. And [specific 3, ideally one about how she works with others]." Fill these from your interview notes before the call.

**What she'd own.** "You told us you want to own a product area, not take tickets. In your first six months you'd own [product area], from talking to installers to shipping it. With five engineers today, that area is yours, not shared across a big team." Name it concretely; it is her top criterion.

**Runway, because she asked twice.** "You asked about runway. Straight answer: we raised $14M in March, and at our current burn that gives us [N] months. [One line on what you plan to do with it.] If you want, our CFO will walk you through it in more detail this week." Don't predict the Series B or its timing.

**The numbers.** "Here's the offer. Base salary of $175,000. A $10,000 sign-on bonus, because we know leaving before February has a cost. 12,000 stock options, which is about 0.12% of the company, vesting over four years with a one-year cliff. Health, dental and vision 90% covered, a $1,500 home office stipend, and a 401k. And a start date of November 30, right after Thanksgiving, as you asked."

Then stop talking.

**The question.** "What's your reaction?" Ask for her reaction, not a decision.

**If she asks for time.** "Of course. I'll send the details in writing this afternoon. Could we aim for a decision by Friday, October 9? [honest reason]. What would you need from us before then to decide? Happy to set up the CFO call or a chat with one of the engineers."

**Don't say:** anything about an equity refresh, the Series B, a future title, or what the options might be worth.

## 5. Offer letter checklist

General information as of 2026-10, not legal advice. Ana works in California, so California rules apply whatever your HQ state. Have counsel review the template once, then reuse it.

**Put in**
- Title (Senior Full-Stack Engineer), manager, full-time, "remote, based in California"
- Start date 2026-11-30, "or another date we agree"
- Base $175,000 annual (or the agreed figure) and pay frequency
- Exempt classification; counsel confirms it meets California's tests for this role and salary
- 12,000 options, type (ISO or NSO) if known, 4-year vest, 1-year cliff, "subject to approval by the Board and the terms of the company's equity plan and grant agreement"; strike price set by the board at grant, no number quoted
- Sign-on: amount and when paid. Recommended: no repayment condition. If you want one, counsel drafts it as a separate agreement that meets AB 692 (Labor Code 926; Business and Professions Code 16608): at least 5 business days to consult a lawyer, prorated, no interest, retention period of 2 years or less, her option to take the bonus at the end of the period instead, and repayment only if she quits or is fired for misconduct
- Benefits in one or two lines, "as described in the plan documents, which may change"
- Contingencies: reference checks; background check only if you run one, with separate written disclosure and authorization, and no conviction-history questions before the conditional offer (Fair Chance Act, Government Code 12952, if you have 5 or more employees, which you do); proof of identity and work authorization on Form I-9 within 3 business days of the start date; signing the confidentiality and invention assignment agreement
- Invention assignment with the written notice required by Labor Code 2872 (the carve-out in 2870 for inventions made entirely on her own time and equipment, unrelated to your business)
- At-will statement where at-will employment applies; counsel confirms the wording
- Integration line in counsel's wording ("this letter and the agreements referenced in it are the complete offer")
- How to accept and the decision date, 2026-10-09
- Remote expenses: Labor Code 2802 requires reimbursing necessary business expenses, including those of working remotely (for example a share of internet and phone). Counsel confirms the $1,500 stipend plus any ongoing reimbursement covers the real cost.

**Leave out**
- Any reference to her current pay, bonus or equity
- The 12-month non-compete (Business and Professions Code 16600.1 and 16600.5)
- A Texas governing-law or venue clause as a condition of employment (Labor Code 925); counsel checks the template
- The Series B equity refresh, or any future grant, raise or title review
- Words that promise permanence or certainty: "permanent", "long-term", "job security", raises or bonuses as certain
- A dollar value for the options or an expected exit price
- Citizenship requirements (only work authorization, through the I-9 after the offer)
- Anything about health, family, age or other protected traits

**Before the start date:** if you don't already run payroll for employees in California, register there now. Counsel or your payroll provider confirms.

## 6. Equity in plain words (send to the candidate)

> **Your grant:** 12,000 stock options, which is about 0.12% of the company today (12,000 divided by about 10,000,000 fully diluted shares, meaning every share and option that exists or is promised). [We'll confirm the exact fully diluted share count before you sign.]
>
> **Vesting:** you earn the options over 4 years. Nothing vests for the first 12 months (the "cliff"); on your 1-year anniversary 25% (3,000 options) vests at once, then the rest vests monthly, 250 options a month for the next 36 months. If you leave before the cliff, you leave with none.
>
> **Strike price:** options are the right to buy shares at a fixed price, set by the board at grant based on an independent valuation (a "409A"). The strike for your grant is [ask us; set by the board at grant]. If the strike is S and you vest 3,000 options, buying them costs 3,000 x S; buying all 12,000 costs 12,000 x S. You only gain if the shares are later worth more than that.
>
> **If you leave:** you usually have [90 days, per our plan; we'll confirm] to buy vested options, or they expire.
>
> **Dilution:** when we raise money, new shares are created, so your percentage goes down. The number of options stays the same. A rough rule: each priced round dilutes everyone, often by the size of the stake sold to new investors.
>
> **Why the percentage today is not a dollar value:** investors usually hold preferred shares that are paid back first in a sale. Common shares (what options become) get what is left. An example, only to show the math: if the company sold for $100M and nothing were owed to preferred holders, 0.12% would be $120,000 before taxes and the cost to exercise, and before any dilution from future rounds. Real outcomes depend on the sale price, preferences and dilution, and many startups return nothing to common holders.
>
> **Taxes:** incentive stock options (ISOs) and non-qualified options (NSOs) are taxed differently [your grant type: ask us], and early exercise or restricted stock can involve an 83(b) election, which must be filed with the IRS within 30 days of receiving the shares. This is not tax advice; talk to a tax advisor about your situation.

## 8. Counteroffer and deadline

**In the pre-close (question 6):** "If your current company offered you more to stay, what would you do?" If she hesitates: "What made you start looking in the first place?" Write her answer down word for word.

**Resignation rehearsal (after she says yes):** "Can we talk through how you'll tell your manager? What do you think they'll say? If they put a number on the table, what will you tell them?" Let her rehearse her reasons out loud. Suggest she resign in person or on video, thank them, and not invite a counter ("I've made my decision and I'm excited about it").

**If a counter arrives:** "Thanks for telling me. When we spoke, you said you were looking because [her words, e.g. you wanted to own a product area, not take tickets]. Does their offer change that?" Re-open your number only if your offer was below the band you set. It isn't, so you don't bid. If she stays, wish her well and keep the door open.

**If the big company comes in higher:** compare on her criteria, not the number. You can go to $185k base and $15k sign-on; above that, let her choose.

**Deadline extension rule:** the decision date is Friday 2026-10-09. If she asks for more time with a concrete reason (her other offer is due that week or the next), extend once to a specific date, for example Friday 2026-10-16, and confirm it in writing. No second extension without a new concrete reason. Never invent a competing finalist to create pressure.
